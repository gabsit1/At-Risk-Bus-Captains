==============================================================================
             AT-RISK BUS CAPTAIN STUDY - RESULTS IN PLAIN ENGLISH             
==============================================================================

==============================================================================
1. WHAT WE WANTED TO FIND OUT
==============================================================================
Every bus captain's vehicle records alarms during the month (harsh braking,
speeding, fatigue, forward collision warnings and so on). We asked: can these
alarms help us spot captains who are more likely to be involved in an
accident, so that the company can offer them coaching BEFORE something
happens?

==============================================================================
2. WHAT DATA WE USED
==============================================================================
  - 1,544 bus captains with alarm records in March 2024.
  - 161 of them (10 out of every 100) also appear in the accident records
    (January to May 2024, after removing duplicates and near-misses).
  - So we are checking: do the captains who had accidents tend to be the ones
    who set off more alarms?

==============================================================================
3. THE SHORT ANSWER
==============================================================================
A SMALL LINK, BUT TOO WEAK TO TRUST FOR INDIVIDUALS. Captains who set off more
alarms are slightly more likely to have had an accident, so the alarms are
worth watching. But the link is modest: it is a nudge, not a crystal ball.

Important: the alarms alone cannot tell us WHICH individual captain will have
an accident. Many captains with lots of alarms never had an accident, and many
with few alarms did. Use the results to prioritise who to check in on first -
never to punish or label someone.

==============================================================================
4. HOW WELL DOES THE ALARM-BASED RISK SCORE WORK?
==============================================================================
We ranked every captain by a risk score built from their alarm rates. Then we
looked at who actually had accidents:

  - If we picked 100 captains at random, about 10 would have had an accident.
  - If we picked the 10% of captains (about 154) with the HIGHEST alarm-based
    risk scores, about 11 in 100 would have had an accident (1.1 times the
    random rate). That is only a small improvement.
  - The 'High risk' group used in the watchlist (it blends the alarm score
    with the computer model) looks stronger: 18 in 100 had an accident,
    compared with 9 in 100 in the 'Low risk' group.
  - Be careful with that last comparison: we measured it on the same captains
    used to build the groups, not on fresh data, so part of the gap may be
    luck. The alarm-only numbers above are the safer ones to quote.

Another way to say it: if you take one captain who had an accident and one who
did not, the one with the accident has the higher risk score 56% of the time.
A coin flip would be 50%, a perfect system 100%. (Plausible range given our
sample size: 51% to 60%.)

==============================================================================
5. WHAT ABOUT THE MACHINE-LEARNING MODELS?
==============================================================================
We also trained computer models (Gradient Boosting, Logistic Regression,
Random Forest) that learn patterns from all the alarm measures at once. We
tested them the proper way: train on 70% of captains, then test on the 30% the
model had never seen, repeated 30 times.

  - The best model (Random Forest) ranked the accident captain higher 56% of
    the time (coin flip = 50%).
  - Among the 10% of captains it rated riskiest, 12 in 100 had an accident
    (random picking: 10 in 100).
  - A shuffle test (we scrambled who had accidents and re-ran the model) shows
    these results are NOT clearly better than luck.
  - When we tested only on accidents that happened AFTER March (76 captains),
    the model scored 49% - essentially a coin flip.

In plain terms: the fancy models did NOT beat the simple alarm score. That is
a normal and honest result when there are only a few hundred accident cases
and one month of alarm data. We recommend the simple, explainable score for
now.

==============================================================================
6. WHICH BEHAVIOURS MATTER MOST?
==============================================================================
For each alarm type on its own, how often does the captain with the accident
have the higher alarm rate? (50% = no link at all.)

  forward collision warnings      57.9%   (link)
  harsh braking                   56.2%   (link)
  following too closely           53.7%   (no clear link)
  fatigue alerts                  53.0%   (no clear link)
  harsh acceleration              52.9%   (no clear link)

The most telling behaviour was forward collision warnings. Behaviours marked
'no clear link' did not reliably help predict accidents in this data.

==============================================================================
8. THINGS TO KEEP IN MIND
==============================================================================
  - Alarms measure driving style and alertness. Accidents are also caused by
    road conditions, other drivers and bad luck - things no alarm can see.
  - We only have one month of alarms, so one bad week can distort a captain's
    score.
  - We do not know how many kilometres each captain drove. We used the number
    of hours with alarm activity as a stand-in, which is not perfect.
  - Captains with fewer than 5 working days were left out of the watchlist
    because their scores would be unreliable.
  - The accident records cover January to May but the alarms only cover March,
    so the timing does not line up perfectly.
  - Treat results as a starting point for coaching and support, not as a
    judgement of any individual.

==============================================================================
9. RECOMMENDED NEXT STEPS
==============================================================================
  - Collect several more months of alarm data - this is the single biggest way
    to improve accuracy.
  - Record accident severity and who was at fault, so we can focus on
    preventable accidents.
  - Record kilometres or hours driven for each captain.
  - Pilot the watchlist as a coaching tool, then check whether flagged
    captains improve.
  - Re-run this study each quarter and compare.

==============================================================================
GLOSSARY
==============================================================================
  - Alarm rate: how many alarms a captain triggers per hour of driving, so
    busy and quiet captains are compared fairly.
  - Risk score: a number from 0 to 100 combining a captain's alarm rates.
    Higher = more alarms than most colleagues.
  - Random picking: choosing captains by chance, with no information - the
    standard we must beat.
  - Coin flip (50%): the score you would get by guessing; anything near 50%
    means no real predictive power.
